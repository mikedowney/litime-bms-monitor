Alarm & status timeline

Built from every stored sample rather than the downsampled chart data, so a brief alarm cannot slip between points.
  • Red — a protection or failure bit was set. Investigate immediately; the event table below gives the raw flag values.
  • Blue — passive balancing active. Normal and desirable near the top of charge above 3.50 V per cell. Balancing during discharge or at low state of charge would be abnormal.
  • Grey — the pack stopped reporting. This is a Bluetooth or collector problem, not a battery fault. Brief gaps are routine; a pack grey for hours needs attention, because you are blind to it rather than because it has failed.
  • Red — discharge is disabled on that pack, so it is electrically isolated and carries none of the load while the others take its share. This is set by the discharge switch in the LiTime app and persists until switched back on. The BMS reports no protection or failure bit for it and the cells look perfectly healthy, which is exactly why it needs its own indicator. A pack that is merely full and has stopped accepting charge is not flagged here — that is normal.
  • Grey on all four packs at once points at the collector service or the GX Bluetooth adapter, not the batteries.

Cell spread (max − min), mV

Spread is the single best indicator of pack health, but it means different things at different points on the charge curve, so the shaded bands move with the bank's condition.
  • Plateau (below 3.45 V/cell): limits 30 and 60 mV. LiFePO4 is electrically flat here, so imbalance is compressed and hard to see. A large spread on the plateau is more concerning than the same number at the top of charge.
  • Top of charge (above 3.45 V/cell): limits 50 and 100 mV. This is where imbalance actually reveals itself, and the most meaningful place to compare packs.
  • Under load (above 20 A): limits 80 and 120 mV. Differences in cell internal resistance dominate, so the reading says more about resistance than about capacity balance.
  • A single excursion means little. A pack trending upward at the same operating point over weeks is the real signal, and usually indicates one cell losing capacity relative to its siblings.
  • Above roughly 160 mV a pack is conventionally considered imbalanced and would benefit from a long absorption hold to let passive balancing work.

Current per pack, A

Positive is charging, negative is discharging. Four identical packs in parallel should share current almost equally.
  • Deadband: the BMS cannot resolve below about ±2.07 A. A reading of exactly 0.0 A means "under 2 A", never "disconnected". Ignore this chart entirely at low bank currents.
  • Healthy sharing is within roughly 3% of each other under meaningful load. Sustained deviation beyond 10% suggests a cable, terminal or fuse with elevated resistance on the odd pack — check torque and look for heat at the connections.
  • One pack at zero while others carry load means its FET is open. At the top of charge this is normal: a full pack stops accepting. During bulk charge or discharge it is not, and it forces the remaining packs to carry proportionally more. Check the FET state row on the pack card to tell the two apart.
  • Capacity check: each pack is rated 200 A continuous, so the bank can carry 800 A with all four online. With only two online a 236 A load puts 118 A through each — still within rating. You do not exceed a pack's limit until three are offline.
  • Compare the sum of all four against the SmartShunt. A persistent shortfall means a pack is contributing without reporting, or has dropped out.

FET state

Two tracks per pack, drawn from the flag word the BMS reports. Green means the FET is conducting normally; a coloured bar means it is open; pale grey means no sample was collected.
  • Charge (amber) — the pack considers itself full and has stopped accepting. Normal, and every healthy pack does it at the top of charge. What is not normal is a pack showing this continuously for days while the others cycle: that pack believes it is permanently full and will never take a charge.
  • Discharge (red) — the pack is isolated and carries none of the load. Set by the discharge switch in the LiTime app and persistent until switched back. Always investigate.
  • Compare against the current chart. A pack showing neither bar but still reading 0.0 A under real load is the awkward case: its FETs report closed yet it is not participating, which points at something other than FET state.
  • The BMS sets no protection or failure bit for either condition, so this chart is the only place these appear.

Pack terminal voltage, V

Voltage measured at each pack's own terminals. Because the packs are hard-paralleled, the true voltage at the busbar is identical for all four at any instant — so the spread you see here is measurement artifact, not a real electrical difference.
  • Expect 50–150 mV of apparent spread. Two causes: each BMS has its own ADC calibration, and the packs are sampled up to two minutes apart, so during active charging the bank has genuinely moved between readings. Neither indicates a problem.
  • Do not use pack-to-pack differences here to infer anything. For imbalance, use the cell spread chart; for participation, use the current chart.
  • What is useful is the shape. All four should rise and fall together. A pack whose line diverges in trend — flattening while others climb, or vice versa — has stopped following the bus, which means its FET has opened.
  • Reference points: 13.6 V float, 13.9 V absorption as configured on this vessel, 14.4 V the manufacturer's rating. Resting near 13.3–13.5 V means a full, settled bank; under 13.0 V at rest means genuinely low.
  • Compare against the SmartShunt rather than between packs. A pack reading persistently more than ~200 mV from the shunt at rest has a calibration issue worth noting, though it affects nothing operationally.

State of charge, %

Each pack reports its own state of charge, counted internally from current in and out. The packs disagreeing with each other is normal and expected, not a fault — they are hard-paralleled, so their true state of charge is identical by definition. What you are seeing is four independent counters drifting apart.
  • Why they drift: the BMS cannot measure below about 2.07 A, so at a dock the fridge and freezer cycling draw roughly 1.5 A per pack and go completely uncounted. The error accumulates in one direction, day after day.
  • The SmartShunt is authoritative for bank state of charge. These figures are diagnostic, never a basis for deciding how much capacity is left.
  • Counters reset at full charge. All four have been observed snapping back to 100% simultaneously once absorption reaches the top. A pack that fails to reset while the others do is the interesting case, and suggests it is not reaching full.
  • SoC drift on the pack cards is a different measure: it compares the reported figure against the BMS's own remaining ÷ capacity arithmetic. Under 1% is normal. Values above 10% mean that pack's counter has overrun and badly needs a full charge.
  • Charging to 14.2 V roughly once a quarter recalibrates every counter at once and lets balancing run. At the 13.9 V setting used here, neither happens.

Cell voltage range, V

Two lines per pack: its highest and lowest cell. The gap between them is the spread charted above.
  • 3.65 V is the practical ceiling for LiFePO4. Cells held above this age quickly, and the BMS will trip overvoltage protection somewhere near 3.75 V.
  • 2.50 V is the floor. Below this you are removing capacity permanently. In normal use you should never approach it; 3.00 V under load already means the bank is nearly empty.
  • 3.50 V is where passive balancing engages. At 13.9 V absorption the cells top out near 3.475 V and never balance, which is a deliberate trade on this vessel.
  • Watch for one pack's maximum running away from the others as charging finishes. That pack will hit protection first and drop out, which is the usual first symptom of a weakening cell.
  • The flat plateau between roughly 3.20 and 3.40 V covers most of the usable capacity, which is why resting voltage is a poor proxy for state of charge.

Temperature, °C

Solid lines are cell temperature. The packs sit in the same compartment, so they should track closely.
  • Charging below 0 °C causes lithium plating, which is permanent and a genuine safety issue. The BMS should block it, but this chart is how you verify that it did. Discharging at sub-zero temperatures is comparatively harmless.
  • Above 45 °C ageing accelerates markedly. Above 55 °C the BMS should be reducing or stopping current, and sustained operation there shortens pack life considerably.
  • Pack-to-pack deviation above 5 °C is the number to watch. Identical packs sharing a compartment and current should be within a couple of degrees. A consistently hotter pack is either carrying more current, has higher internal resistance, or has restricted airflow — all worth investigating.
  • MOSFET temperature normally runs a few degrees above cell temperature under load and converges when idle. A gap that widens over months at the same current suggests the FETs or an internal connection are degrading.
  • Temperature rising while current is near zero is abnormal and warrants prompt physical inspection.

Poll reliability (24 h)

Occasional Bluetooth aborts are normal in a compartment full of competing 2.4 GHz devices. Above 90% is healthy. A single pack far below its siblings usually means signal or contention rather than a battery problem; all four falling together points at the collector or the adapter.

Internal resistance

Derived, not measured directly: the difference between the sum of the cell voltages and the terminal voltage is fitted against current, and the slope is the resistance of the FETs, shunt, busbars and welds between them.
  • 0.2–0.4 mΩ is normal for a healthy 320 Ah pack. The absolute figure matters far less than how it moves.
  • A pack rising relative to its siblings over months is the signal — loosening terminals, fatiguing welds, or ageing FETs. This is the earliest warning you are likely to get.
  • Only samples above 10 A count. Below that the BMS cannot resolve current, and a pack with an open FET reports zero while its cells float free — both would corrupt the fit.
  • Low R² is expected, not a fault. The calibration offset between the two measurement chains is noisier than the signal, so the figure only becomes meaningful over hundreds of samples.
  • It only accumulates on days the bank is actually used. At the dock nothing here will change.

Capacity & wear

Learned capacity is each BMS's own estimate, refined as it observes full charge and discharge cycles. Absolute values matter less than divergence over months — a pack steadily losing capacity relative to its siblings is ageing faster. Cycle counts that drift apart mean the packs are doing unequal work, which is worth understanding even when nothing is faulty.
